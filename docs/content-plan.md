22 Sep 2026 · a plan, not the articles themselves · nothing here is written yet

The structure comes from `docs/positioning.md` §6: **one hub, four spokes, and one page that
is not an article.** This document turns that into a publishing plan — what each piece is
for, who it is for, what it must contain to be worth reading, what blocks it, and the order
I would publish in.

## What each piece has to carry

An article that explains a concept gets read. An article that hands over an **artefact** gets
used, cited and remembered. Each piece below is built around one.

### Hub — the guide

**Reader:** an owner or managing director of a company of 50–300 people, who has heard
"governance" from an investor, a bank or a large client and is not sure whether it applies at
their size.

**Must contain**
- Governance defined as *who decides what, to which limit, and on what record* — not as a board
- The four or five moments that make an SME need it: an investor question, a client
  questionnaire, a bank facility, a founder who cannot sign everything, a family handover
- What it is not: not a legal service, not a certificate, not software
- A short self-assessment an owner can answer honestly in five minutes
- Where each of the five services fits, and honest signposting to the four spokes

**Length:** the longest piece, 2,000–2,500 words. It is the page everything else points at.

### Spoke 1 — Delegation of authority

**Artefact:** the **delegation of authority matrix**. The article should show a real one —
decision type, who decides, financial limit, what record is kept, who is informed — filled in
for a plausible SME, and explain how to build one.

**Must contain:** the failure mode (everything routed to one signature), the four columns and
what belongs in each, three or four worked rows, how limits are set, where the matrix is kept
and how it is reviewed, and what changes for the owner on the Monday after.

**Length:** 1,200–1,600 words.

### Spoke 2 — The anti-bribery questionnaire

**Artefact:** a mapping of what the questionnaire is actually asking for. Most questions
reduce to four things: **a policy, a procedure, a record, and a named responsible person.**
This is the most immediately useful piece in the set, because it answers a question someone is
asking with a deadline attached.

**Must contain:** what triggers a questionnaire, the four things behind almost every question,
the common questions with what an honest answer looks like, what to do when the honest answer
is "we don't have that yet" (usually the practice exists and the documentation does not), and
the line beyond which this needs a lawyer or an accredited certifier.

**Careful:** ISO 37001 must be described precisely — readiness, implementation and
certification are three different things, and no consultancy can certify a company it advises.
The service pages already get this right; the article must match.

**Length:** 1,400–1,800 words.

### Spoke 3 — Strategy into decision rights

**Artefact:** the **monthly review**. Its agenda, who owns each measure, what happens when a
measure is missed twice.

**Must contain:** why strategies stall (nobody owns anything, nothing is reviewed on a
rhythm), how an objective becomes an owner plus a measure plus a decision right, a worked
example, the review agenda, and how this ties back to the delegation matrix in Spoke 1.

**Length:** 1,200–1,600 words.

### Spoke 4 — Employee AI use

**Artefact:** a one-page **acceptable use statement** an SME can adopt and adapt.

**Must contain:** what staff are already doing with AI on company information, the three or
four rules worth writing down first, what not to put into a public tool, who approves
exceptions, and how to review it as tools change. Scope stays deliberately narrow — this tests
interest in AI governance without making AI the firm's identity.

**Blocked:** this spoke publishes only if AI Governance has a named delivery lead. The service
page is written and hidden for exactly the same reason. If no credible lead exists, replace
this spoke rather than publish it thin.

### The page — How an engagement works

**Not an article.** It answers, for the whole firm: how an engagement is scoped, what the
first conversation covers, what the client provides, what they receive, how long it typically
runs, how it is priced, and what happens at the end.

**Blocked:** it carries verified commercial terms or it does not publish. This is the single
most-asked question on any consultancy site, and the one the audit flagged as a failure.
Inventing the answer would be worse than leaving it out.

## The order I would publish in

The temptation is to write the hub first because everything links to it. I would not.

| Order | Piece | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Spoke 2 — the questionnaire** | The highest-intent question in the set. Someone searching it has a deadline. It needs no facts the firm doesn't already hold, and it proves the format works |
| 2 | **Spoke 1 — delegation of authority** | The other priority situation, and the firm's core artefact. Together, 1 and 2 cover both sentences the homepage leads with |
| 3 | **The hub** | Written after two spokes exist, so it links to real pages rather than promises — and so its scope is informed by what the spokes actually turned out to say |
| 4 | **`/how-we-work`** | The moment commercial terms are confirmed. It converts the readers the first three pieces bring |
| 5 | **Spoke 3 — strategy into decision rights** | Real demand, but less urgent than 1 and 2 |
| 6 | **Spoke 4 — employee AI use** | Last, and only with a named lead |

At two pieces a month, the set is complete in about three months. At one a month, six. **I
would rather see one good piece a month than two thin ones** — the thin ones are what AI
assistants ignore.

## What blocks what

| Blocker | Blocks | Who resolves it |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning sign-off | Everything | Founder |
| Named delivery leads for the five services | Nothing here strictly, but every article links to a service page currently advertising "needs delivery lead" | Founder |
| Verified commercial terms | `/how-we-work` | Founder |
| A named AI Governance lead | Spoke 4 | Founder |
| Qualified reviewer for any named Egyptian or Gulf law or regulator | Spoke 2 mainly, and parts of the hub | Founder to nominate |
| Who reviews and approves each article before publishing | All of them | Founder |

The review point is the one most often skipped. Positioning §10 (A7) asks for a realistic
publishing capacity **and a named reviewer**. A hub-and-spoke set only works if the spokes keep
coming; two published and four abandoned is worse than none, because the hub advertises the
gaps.

## The three articles already on the site

`/blog` currently carries three AI articles: human oversight of AI decisions, AI bias in MENA
education, and AI scaling institutional bias. They are competent, and they are the only
published content.

They are also **off-position**. None answers a question a governance buyer is asking, and one
is about education rather than SMEs. Left as they are, a visitor's first impression from the
blog is a firm that writes about AI ethics.

Three options:

1. **Leave them.** They do no harm and they show the site is alive. Once the hub and two
   spokes exist, they become a minority of the blog rather than all of it. *(My recommendation.)*
2. **Re-anchor them.** Add a line to each connecting it to governance, and link it to the hub
   once the hub exists. Cheap, and it makes them part of the set rather than strays.
3. **Retire them.** Only if the founder decides AI commentary is not something the firm wants
   to be known for. This costs the only three pieces of content the site has.

I would do 1 now, and 2 when the hub publishes.
