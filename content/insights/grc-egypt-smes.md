Many owners of small and mid-sized companies in Egypt still think of governance, risk and compliance (GRC) as something for listed companies and banks. The assumption made sense when growth came mainly from the owner's own network and the company's own cash.

That is changing. When an SME applies for serious financing, bids to become a supplier to a large company or a government body, or sits down with an international partner, it is increasingly asked questions it cannot answer from memory. Who decides what? How are risks identified? Which laws apply to you, and how do you know you comply? Can you show it on paper?

GRC is simply the discipline of having good answers to those questions. It does not have to be heavy or expensive. It does have to be real.

## What GRC means in practice for an SME

The three letters cover three related ideas.

**Governance** is how the company is directed and controlled: who owns it, who sits on the board or acts as manager, which decisions are reserved for owners, and which are delegated to management.

**Risk management** is how the company identifies what could go wrong, decides which risks matter most, and assigns someone to act on them.

**Compliance** is how the company knows which legal, regulatory and contractual obligations apply to it, and how it meets them consistently rather than by chance.

In a founder-led business, all three often live in one person's head. That works until the founder is unavailable, a key manager leaves, a lender asks for documents, or a client's audit team arrives. GRC moves that knowledge into simple structures that others can see and rely on.

## Why the pressure is growing

**Lenders and investors.** Banks, funds and strategic investors carry out due diligence before they commit money. Beyond the financial statements, they look at ownership clarity, decision rights, related-party dealings, key-person dependence and legal exposures. A company that can produce clear answers quickly is easier to finance and usually negotiates from a stronger position.

**Large clients and government buyers.** Large companies increasingly ask suppliers to confirm compliance with codes of conduct, anti-bribery rules, data protection and labour standards, and some audit their suppliers. Missing policies or inconsistent records can remove an SME from a shortlist before price is discussed.

**International partners.** Foreign distributors, licensors and joint-venture partners must manage their own legal and reputational exposure. They look for counterparts whose controls they can trust.

**Specific Egyptian obligations.** Several frameworks now reach well beyond large corporations. Three examples are worth knowing:

- **Personal data.** The [Personal Data Protection Law No. 151 of 2020](https://eg.andersen.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Law-No.-151-OF-2020-1.pdf) applies to organizations that process personal data, which includes almost every business with customers and employees. Its executive regulations were issued by [Ministerial Decree No. 816 of 2025](https://cms.law/en/are/legal-updates/egypt-s-pdpl-executive-regulations-issued-one-year-compliance-countdown-begins), with a formal date of 1 November 2025. Major law firms expect the one-year grace period to end on [1 November 2026](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/egypt-important-data-protection-update/). The regulations introduce requirements such as licences or permits from the Personal Data Protection Center, a registered data protection officer, records of processing, Arabic privacy notices and [notification of personal data breaches to the Center within 72 hours](https://cms.law/en/are/legal-updates/egypt-s-pdpl-executive-regulations-issued-one-year-compliance-countdown-begins). Licence fees are tiered by the number of records held, and [entities holding up to 100,000 records are reported to be exempt from the fee](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/egypt-important-data-protection-update/). Check the Center's current guidance for how each requirement applies to your company.
- **Anti-money laundering.** [Law No. 80 of 2002](https://eg.andersen.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Law-No.-80-of-2002.pdf) covers financial institutions and also certain non-financial businesses and professions, including real estate brokers, dealers in precious metals and gemstones in cash transactions, and lawyers and accountants when carrying out specified transactions for clients. If you operate in one of these activities, customer due diligence and reporting obligations apply. If you do not, your banks will still apply them to you, so expect "know your customer" questions.
- **Employment.** The new [Labour Law No. 14 of 2025](https://www.ey.com/en_gl/technical/tax-alerts/egypt-enacts-new-labor-law-with-changes-affecting-employers-beginning-1-september-2025) took effect on 1 September 2025, and a [2026 ministerial decree](https://www.mondaq.com/employee-rights-labour-relations/1842704/egypts-new-model-work-regulations-are-now-in-force-what-you-need-to-know) sets out what internal work regulations must cover for private-sector establishments with ten or more employees. We cover this in a separate article on HR compliance.

For good practice on boards and decision rights, the [Egyptian Code of Corporate Governance](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/codes/egyptian-code-of-corporate-governance), issued by the Egyptian Institute of Directors in 2016, is a useful reference even for unlisted companies. Treat it as a guide to principles, not a template to copy in full.

## A proportionate minimum GRC set

An SME does not need the governance apparatus of a bank. It needs a small set of tools that match its size and risks, and that are actually used. We suggest seven.

1. **Ownership and board clarity.** Up-to-date commercial register, articles and shareholder records. A clear statement of who the board or managers are, what they decide, and how often they meet. In family businesses, a short written agreement on how family and business roles are separated.
2. **Delegation of authority (DoA).** A one- or two-page matrix showing who can approve spending, contracts, hiring, pricing exceptions and payments, and up to what limits. This single document removes a large share of everyday control weaknesses.
3. **Core policies.** A short set, written in plain Arabic and English where needed: code of conduct, conflicts of interest and related parties, anti-bribery and gifts, data protection and information security, procurement, and HR. Fewer policies that people read are better than many that nobody opens.
4. **Risk register.** A simple list of the ten to twenty risks that matter most to the business, each with an owner, current controls and next actions, reviewed by management every quarter and by the board at least twice a year.
5. **Compliance calendar.** Every recurring legal, tax, regulatory and contractual deadline in one place, with an owner and a reminder. Licences, filings, renewals, insurance and key client reporting all belong here.
6. **Internal controls over money and data.** Segregation of duties between ordering, approving, receiving and paying. Bank reconciliations, access controls on systems, and a periodic review of user rights.
7. **Speak-up channel.** A confidential way for employees and suppliers to raise concerns, with a named person outside the line of command to receive them and a commitment that nobody is penalized for reporting in good faith.

## A phased roadmap

**Phase 1: Diagnose (four to six weeks).** Map ownership and decision rights as they actually work. List the laws, licences and contracts that apply. Interview the owner and key managers about what keeps them awake at night. The output is a short gap report with priorities, not a long audit.

**Phase 2: Foundations (two to three months).** Put the DoA, compliance calendar and first risk register in place. Fix the most urgent legal gaps, such as data protection steps ahead of the regulatory deadline or employment documentation. Agree a regular rhythm of management and board meetings with minutes.

**Phase 3: Policies and controls (three to six months).** Write or update the core policies, train managers on them, and tighten financial and system controls. Open the speak-up channel and explain it to staff.

**Phase 4: Embed and evidence (ongoing).** Review the risk register quarterly. Test a few controls each year. Keep a simple file of evidence: minutes, approvals, training records, and compliance confirmations. This file is what you will show a lender, investor or client auditor.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Copying a large corporation's policy manual that nobody in an SME can follow.
- Treating GRC as a one-off project for a financing round rather than a way of running the company.
- Giving compliance to a junior employee with no authority.
- Writing policies in a language or style that frontline staff do not use.
- Ignoring sector rules because "nobody has checked yet".

## Governance as a growth asset

Good GRC is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It lets an owner delegate with confidence, makes the business easier to finance and value, and reduces the chance that a single mistake becomes a crisis. For many Egyptian SMEs, it is also becoming the price of entry to larger clients and better capital.

Start small, keep it proportionate, and make it visible. A company that can show how it is run is a company others are more willing to back.
